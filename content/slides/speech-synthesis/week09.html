Normalization, before scoring

The normalizer is part of the metric

The normalizer
  • Fixed rules applied to reference and hypothesis before scoring
  • Each rule changes the WER
  • Two papers with different normalizers report different quantities, both called WER
In practice
  • Whisper ships its own normalizer: it turns “two” into “2”, the opposite of the class rule
  • Either rule works if both sides get the same one
  • In A1: fix yours before seeing results; state it in the report
Limits of WER

What WER cannot see

NO SPACES, NO WORDS

Use CER (character error rate)

  • Same formula, over characters
  • Mandarin and Japanese text has no spaces between words
  • One Singapore code-switched line can mix spaced English words and unspaced Chinese characters
EVERY ERROR COSTS 1

Meaning does not count

  • “gonna” → “gotta” and “gonna” → “banana”: 1 error each
  • “gonna” → “going to”: 2 errors (1 substitution + 1 insertion)
  • Dropping “lah” and dropping “not”: 1 error each
WHOSE ORTHOGRAPHY?

Reference text is a choice

  • Is the reference “going to” or “gonna”? “makan” or a translation?
  • The corpus's transcription conventions become the definition of correct
Rule of the day
  • Fix the normalizer before you look at any WER
  • Use the same one for both systems
  • Report it with the reference conventions and the test population
Illustrative numbers · one number hides two

Split the average

  • overall WER = w1 × WER on the majority accent + w2 × WER on code-switched speech, where each w is the group's share of the reference words (here w1 = 0.75, w2 = 0.25)
  • A different test-set mix can change which system has the lower overall WER, with no change to either model
  • Disaggregation: WER reported separately for each named group of speakers, with utterance and word counts
Averaging WER · illustrative numbers

Two ways to average WER

Pooled WER
WERpooled=Σi EiΣi Ni
  • Total errors divided by total reference words: long utterances count more
  • What jiwer returns for a list of utterances
Mean of per-utterance WERs
WERmean=1UΣiEiNi
  • Each utterance's WER first, then their average: every utterance counts equally, however short

Ei: errors in utterance i   Ni: reference words in utterance i   U: number of utterances   Σi: add up over the utterances

Example: utterance 1 has 2 words and 2 errors; utterance 2 has 18 words and 0 errors
POOLED
2 + 02 + 18=220=10%
MEAN
12(22+018)=12(100% + 0%)=50%
  • Report which average you use, with utterances and reference words for every cell
  • Pooled over groups, it is the weighting in Split the average: each group's WER × its share of the words

The published audit · Koenecke et al. (2020)

Five recognizers, one racial gap: 35% vs 19% WER

  • Five commercial recognizers (Amazon, Apple, Google, IBM, Microsoft) transcribing interview snippets
  • Black speakers from CORAAL (Corpus of Regional African American Language), white speakers from Voices of California: 2,141 snippets per group, matched on age, gender and duration
  • Average WER 35% vs 19%; all five systems show the gap
  • On 206 short phrases spoken by both groups, WER was still about twice as large for Black speakers
  • Among Black speakers, WER rose with dialect density (the share of African American English features in the speech)
WHY IDENTICAL PHRASES MATTER
  • Same phrases give both groups the same words and the same language-model prior
  • The remaining gap points to how the words were pronounced and heard: the acoustic model and its training audio

The authors: the gap arises “primarily from a performance gap in the acoustic models”.

Koenecke et al. (2020), PNAS 117(14), 7684–7689 (quoted).

Diagnosis, in this course's vocabulary

Where a WER gap can come from

LEXICON

Missing words or pronunciations

  • Names, loanwords, particles absent from the vocabulary
  • A word listed with one pronunciation only (the Singapore English /ɛ/ and /æ/ merger)
  • Matched phrases rule out missing words, not missing pronunciations
LANGUAGE MODEL

Improbable word sequences

  • A language model that gives one group's grammar low probability
  • Controlled by matching content
  • Still a possible cause for code-switching and particles in Singapore
ACOUSTIC MODEL

Voices missing from the training audio

  • Vowel spaces, prosody and voice quality the training audio never contained
  • The matched-phrase evidence points here
  • Only data changes it
  • Week 1's sentence, now explained: the system learned someone's phonology; an audit shows whose
  • End-to-end model: all three sit inside one network trained on one dataset
  • So the remaining option is better training data
The Whisper-era question

Does 680,000 hours of training audio close the gap?

  • Averages improve with scale. Per language, “WER halves for every 16× increase in training data” (Radford et al. 2023)
  • Gaps remain in current systems: J&M Fig. 16.1 lists 16.2% WER on CORAAL interviews against 5.8% to 11% on other conversational English
  • The same audit applies to L2 accents, children's and older voices, and atypical speech. For any “supported language”, ask: which test set, which speakers, what WER?
SUMMARY

Usable is not equitable.

  • A model can be good enough for most users on average
  • It can still have its highest WER on the same groups
  • Only a per-group table shows the good average and the group gap together
The same audit in Singapore

What is the Singapore version of this study?

  • The Singapore axis is language use, not race in the American sense: particles (lah, leh), Singlish grammar, switching languages within a sentence
  • NSC metadata (information stored with each recording: speaker gender and ethnic group, recording channel, corpus part) makes per-group tables possible
  • Read speech vs conversation is itself an axis: Part 3 (conversation) is usually the hardest for recognizers
  • In the practical you build this kind of table on class data; in A1, on your own test set
AUDIT ETHICS
  • A high-WER cell describes the model and its training data, not the speakers in it
  • Metadata categories are administrative, not phonetic: ethnicity is not an accent
  • A cell's WER does not describe any one speaker in it
  • Report which model, which data, which group

N
The corpus

The National Speech Corpus

  • A corpus is audio + transcripts (whose conventions?) + metadata (who is speaking) + licence (who may use it, for what)
  • Commissioned by IMDA (Infocomm Media Development Authority), built with NTU linguists; before it, the largest Singapore English speech corpus had about 125 hours
  • Parts 1 and 2 oversample Malay and Indian speakers: about 60% Chinese, 19% Malay, 19% Indian (citizen population: 74%, 13%, 9%)
  • Singapore Open Data Licence v1.0: research and commercial use, on request; name IMDA and the NSC when you use it
Speaker metadata

What the metadata makes possible

Audits
  • Per-group WER tables become possible: by gender, ethnic group or corpus part
  • Without these columns, no group gap can be measured
Phonetics at scale
  • In Week 3 you measured F1 and F2 of eight vowels by hand
  • With metadata plus forced alignment, the same measurement runs over thousands of speakers
  • Vowel spaces, particles and prosody by group
WHAT IT CANNOT TELL YOU
  • Ethnic group is not language dominance
  • “Part 3” is not one register
  • Metadata labels groups (for example female, Malay), not individual people
  • Speakers within one group can differ as much as the groups do

Age band and education were collected (Koh et al. 2019); check your copy's speaker file before planning an age contrast.

Reading a corpus critically

Four limits of most corpora

Read speech is not conversation

  • Read sentences (NSC Parts 1 and 2) give clean transcripts
  • But they lose reduction, overlap and natural particle use
  • Conversation (NSC Part 3) is the harder test, and the harder to transcribe

Prompted local words

  • Part 2's prompts include local names and food on purpose: Tampines (a town in eastern Singapore), kopi peng (iced coffee with milk)
  • Good for coverage, but not evidence of how often anyone says them

Who is missing

  • Every recruitment method misses some people: ages, occupations, residents who fit no census box
  • Groups missing from the corpus cannot appear in the audit

Consent has a scope

  • NSC speakers were recruited and agreed to a stated use under a licence
  • Whisper's 680,000 hours are audio found on the web, with no record of who the speakers are or what they agreed to
  • Week 12 returns to consent

Check these four points for any corpus before you use it.

Forced alignment is recognition
with the transcript given.

The words are fixed. Viterbi decides only when each phone starts and ends.

V
The classical recognizer, used for measurement

Forced alignment: Viterbi, with the words known

  • Recognition asked: which words? Alignment already knows the words and asks only: when?
  • Week 6's machinery without the language model: lexicon → phones → one chain of phone HMMs → Viterbi places the boundaries
  • Two inputs besides the audio: a pronunciation dictionary and an acoustic model
  • Output: a TextGrid (Praat's annotation file: labelled time intervals on tiers), with a word tier and a phone tier
  • You have made one already: the boundary between /n/ and /o/ in Week 6's backtraced state path
WHY IT IS STILL USED
  • End-to-end models replaced the pipeline for transcription
  • CTC models can align too, but each label peaks on one or two frames between blanks, so phone boundaries are less precise
  • Measurement needs boundaries from a documented, repeatable method: HMM aligners such as MFA remain standard in phonetic research
Worked example (illustrative): “kopi”, frames x1 to x20

Viterbi places the phone boundaries

  • The path may only stay in a phone or move to the next: the word is fixed, so the only freedom is when each phone ends
  • Viterbi fills the whole table first; the backtrace from x20 gives this path, drawn left to right
Worked example (illustrative): “kopi”, 4 phones, 8 frames

The table behind the path, with numbers

V(s, t) = P(xt | s) × max[ V(s, t−1), V(s−1, t−1) ]
  • s: a phone, in the word's order; the path can only stay in s or move on from s−1
  • Start in /k/ at x1, end in /i/ at x8; stay and move are equally likely here, so they drop out

The tool, read critically

What MFA needs, and what to distrust

WHAT IT NEEDS
  • Every word in the dictionary: mfa validate lists the missing ones (out-of-vocabulary, OOV)
  • An OOV word like “makan” becomes one block of noise (spn), with no phones inside
  • Fix: add the word to the dictionary yourself
  • Pronunciations must fit the speaker: if a speaker merges /ɛ/ and /æ/, so must the dictionary
WHAT TO DISTRUST
  • Alignments are hypotheses
  • They fail on: missing words, the wrong variety's dictionary, untranscribed hesitations or laughter, code-switches, heavy reduction, overlap
  • Spot-check in Praat before trusting a measurement

The practical gives you a TextGrid made with MFA; running MFA yourself is optional.

In the textbook's words

This week, formally

DEFINITION
Word error rate

From the alignment of the two strings, “the minimum number of word substitutions, word insertions, and word deletions”; the rate “can be greater than 100%”. Course gloss: WER = 100 × (S + D + I) ÷ N, with N the words in the reference.

Jurafsky & Martin, SLP 3rd ed., §16.6 (quoted; Aug 2026 draft).
DEFINITION
Text normalization (for scoring)

“It’s normal for systems to normalize text before computing word error rate.” Course gloss: one fixed clean-up applied to reference and hypothesis (lowercase, punctuation, contractions, numerals); it is part of the metric and is reported with it.

J&M §16.6 (quoted). Whisper's normalizer: Radford et al. (2023), appendix on text standardization.
DEFINITION
Disaggregated evaluation

Course wording: the same metric reported separately for each named group in the test set, with utterance and word counts per group. The standard scoring tool already allows it: “sclite also gives error rates by speaker”.

J&M §16.6 (quoted). Exemplar: Koenecke et al. (2020), PNAS 117(14), 7684–7689.
DEFINITION
Forced alignment

Course wording: given audio and its known transcript, the assignment of time boundaries to the words and phones, by running the recognizer's Viterbi search over the fixed transcript.

Tool: McAuliffe et al. (2017), Proc. Interspeech 2017, 498–502 (Montreal Forced Aligner).

Before you go

Four things to remember

1. A WER needs its normalizer, its reference conventions and its test population stated beside it

2. An overall WER is a weighted average over groups; report WER for each group as well, with counts

3. In Koenecke et al. (2020) the gap stayed on matched phrases: it points to the acoustic model and its training audio

4. Forced alignment is Viterbi with the words known: a start and end time, so a duration, for every phone

Answers

EX 9.1
  • Raw tokens: 5 S (Can≠can; lah,≠lah; we’re≠we; 2≠two; pm.≠pm) + 1 I (+are) over 7 reference words = 6 ÷ 7 ≈ 86%; going, at ✓
  • After normalization (lowercase, strip punctuation, we’re → we are, 2 → two) the strings are identical: 0%
  • Same audio, same transcript, two numbers: the normalizer is part of the metric
EX 9.2

A(p) = 8p + 25(1 − p) and B(p) = 13p + 15(1 − p), with p = group M's share and 1 − p = group C's share of the reference words. Equal when 25 − 17p = 15 − 2p: 15p = 10, p = 2/3. Above two thirds group M, A has the lower average; below, B does. The models never changed: only the mix did.

EX 9.3

mfa validate flags “makan” as out-of-vocabulary; MFA aligns it as spoken noise (spn): one interval with no boundaries between its phones, so its vowels cannot be measured, and its edges with the neighbouring words are less reliable. Fix: add one tab-separated dictionary line (makan, then its phones in the acoustic model's phone set) and re-align. A fixed lexicon again, but here the fix is one line you add yourself.

EX 9.4

Possible, for example: WER by gender within Part 3; base-vs-fine-tune gains by ethnic group on a part the fine-tune was not trained on (it used Part 2). Not answerable, for example: whether errors track how many Singlish features (particles, Singlish grammar) an utterance contains (no such column: register is not recorded per utterance), or speakers' language dominance (ethnic group is an administrative category, not a linguistic profile). Metadata names cells; it does not describe repertoires.
